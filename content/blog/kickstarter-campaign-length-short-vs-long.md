There's a common piece of Kickstarter advice: run a shorter campaign. It's easy to repeat and hard to verify — so we checked it against Backer Sonar's full dataset of completed campaigns, bucketed by how long each campaign actually ran.

## The numbers

| Duration | Campaigns | Success rate | Median funding multiple |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Short (1–15 days) | 13,196 | 75.6% | 1.38x |
| Medium (16–30 days) | 105,797 | 63.1% | 1.05x |
| Long (31+ days) | 59,871 | 51.5% | 1.00x |

Short campaigns succeed at a meaningfully higher rate (75.6%) than medium-length ones (63.1%), which in turn outperform long campaigns (51.5%). The pattern holds across the full dataset, not just a single category.

## Why this probably isn't just "shorter is better"

Before treating this as a rule, it's worth noting what the data can't tell you:

- **Short campaigns are the minority.** Only 13,196 of the 178,864 completed campaigns in this breakdown ran 15 days or fewer — most creators choose medium-length campaigns (105,797, by far the largest group). Short campaigns being rarer is itself a hint that most creators don't consider them the default choice.
- **Kickstarter itself nudges toward shorter campaigns**, and creators who run 1–15 day campaigns are frequently repeat creators with an existing audience already primed to back on day one — a very different starting position than a first-time creator building an audience during the campaign itself.
- **Urgency and momentum compound.** A shorter runway concentrates backer attention instead of spreading it thin, which can genuinely help — this isn't purely a self-selection artifact, but it's difficult to fully separate "shorter campaigns perform better" from "campaigns run by people confident enough to go short tend to perform better regardless of length."

## What this is useful for

If you're deciding between a 2-week and a 6-week campaign with a similar audience and readiness level, the historical pattern favors shorter. If you're a first-time creator still building an audience, a longer window to reach people may matter more than this pattern alone suggests — the data shows what happened, not what will happen for your specific situation.
